Daily Devotional Verses



Romans 6:12-14

NKJV (New King James Version)

Living Under Grace

12 Therefore do not let sin reign in your mortal body, that you should obey it in its lusts. 13 And do not present your members as instruments of unrighteousness to sin, but present yourselves to God as being alive from the dead, and your members as instruments of righteousness to God. 14 For sin shall not have dominion over you, for you are not under law but under grace.

GUIDE REFLECTION:

Present Yourselves to God as Those Given New Life

In the first part of the verse, Paul commands believers not to present any part of their bodies to sin as instruments of unrighteousness. They no longer have to give themselves over to the former master that once ruled their lives. But the Christian life is not simply about rejecting sin. The freedom we have been given in Christ has a deeper and more positive direction: the person who has been set free from former slavery is now called to present himself to God.

“But present yourselves to God as being alive from the dead.”

The words “But present yourselves” are very important. Paul does not describe the Christian life as one focused only on what we should no longer do. We have not been freed from sin simply so that we can live for ourselves. Our allegiance has changed. The surrender we once gave to sin must now be replaced by a willing surrender to God. True freedom in Christ, then, is not freedom without a Lord; it is the freedom to live for the God who saved us.

To “present yourselves to God” means more than making a one-time decision. It means willingly placing ourselves under His will. This includes our plans, desires, decisions, relationships, time, and the direction of our lives. God is not merely one part of a believer's life whom we turn to when we are in need. He is the Lord who is worthy of our obedience, trust, and wholehearted service.

This is also at the heart of Jesus' call to those who desire to follow Him:

“If anyone desires to come after Me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross daily, and follow Me.” — Luke 9:23

Following Christ is not simply a matter of adding Him to a life we still insist on controlling. His call is to a life that no longer treats our own desires as the highest standard. Presenting ourselves to God means recognizing that we no longer have the final say over the direction of our lives. Our desires and plans are placed under the will of the Lord to whom we belong.

But it is important to see the basis on which Paul gives this command. He does not simply say, “Present yourselves to God.” He adds, “as being alive from the dead.” In other words, we do not present ourselves to God in order to gain new life. We present ourselves because we have already been given new life through our union with Christ. Obedience is not a way of forcing God to accept us; it is the response of those who have already experienced His salvation.

This distinction is extremely important. It is easy to think that we must first become obedient enough to be worthy of God. When our spiritual life seems to be going well, we may feel closer to Him. When we fail, we may think that we first need to prove ourselves before we can draw near again. But that is not the order of the gospel. Paul does not say that we should present ourselves so that we may be made alive. He says that we should present ourselves as those who have been brought from death to life.

Obedience, then, is the fruit of grace, not the price we pay for it. God is the One who acts to save sinners and bring them out of their former condition into new life in Christ. Because of this, surrender becomes a response of gratitude and faith. We do not obey in order to make God ours; we obey because we already belong to Him.

We see this same response elsewhere in Paul's letter to the Romans:

“I beseech you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, that you present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable to God.” — Romans 12:1

Notice that Paul's appeal is grounded in “the mercies of God.” What God has done comes before our response. This is the pattern of the gospel: God saves us by His grace, and the life He has saved is then called to be offered back to Him. Surrender is not a way of repaying God—we could never repay His grace. It is the recognition that the life we have is His gift and that He rightly belongs at the center of it.

This verse challenges us in a deeply personal way. We may want to be rescued from the consequences of sin while still refusing to surrender control of our lives to God. We may desire forgiveness, peace, and the promises of God while still insisting on deciding for ourselves what we will and will not obey. But we have already seen where life leads when sin and our own desires are allowed to govern its direction. Sin promises freedom, but its path leads to destruction, painful consequences, regret, and ruin.

That is why surrendering to God should not be viewed as simply giving up our freedom. It is also an expression of trust in the God who knows far better than we do where our lives should go. The God who calls us to surrender is the same God who desires to lead us away from sin's destructive path and guide us in the way of righteousness. His commands were not given to deprive us of true life, but to keep us from the very things that belong to the slavery from which His grace has already rescued us.

Jesus clearly showed this contrast when He said:

“The thief does not come except to steal, and to kill, and to destroy. I have come that they may have life, and that they may have it more abundantly.” — John 10:10

Sin often presents itself as the path to freedom, pleasure, or fulfillment, but its direction leads us away from the life God desires for us. Christ, on the other hand, came to give life. He is not only the Savior who rescues us from sin's destructive rule; He is also the Good Shepherd who lovingly guides and cares for those who belong to Him.

Jesus said:

“I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd gives His life for the sheep.” — John 10:11

The One who calls us to follow Him is the same One who gave His own life for us. So when God calls us to surrender our lives to Him, He is not leading us toward something worse than what we would choose for ourselves.

Just as sheep trust their shepherd, we can entrust the direction of our lives to Christ because our Good Shepherd knows the right path, cares for His sheep, and has already shown the depth of His love by laying down His life for them.

He leads us away from the destructive rule of sin and into a life under His good and trustworthy care.

👉 We have already seen where life under the rule of sin leads. Surrendering to God means trusting that the God who saved us is also the One we can trust to guide the life He has given us.

This changes the way we view obedience. God's rule is not the rule of a cruel master who wants to take life away from us. It is the loving rule of the God who rescued us from a master that was destroying us. The person who has been brought from death to life is called to recognize God as Lord over his whole being—not because He wants to withhold what is good from us, but because He is our Good Shepherd who knows far better than we do where we need to be led. We may not always understand His ways, but we can trust that His wisdom is greater than ours.

There are times when surrender feels easy because God's will aligns with what we want. It becomes much harder when obedience requires us to...

  • let go of something that matters deeply to us,
  • forgive someone who has hurt us,
  • walk away from a sinful relationship or practice,
  • tell the truth even when it costs us,
  • or trust God when we do not understand what He is doing.

In moments like these, it becomes clear whether our obedience depends only on convenience or whether we truly recognize God as Lord.

In such situations, we may hesitate because we can see the difficulty of obedience but cannot yet see where it will lead. We may wonder why we need to let go, forgive, turn away, or trust when remaining with what is familiar seems easier. But when something is clearly in accordance with God's will and His Word, we do not need to understand everything before we obey.

Obedience is an expression of trust: we believe that God sees the road ahead more clearly than we can see it now.

We see a clear example of this in the experience of the Israelites in Egypt. When God declared that He had seen their suffering and would deliver them from slavery, they may have expected their circumstances to improve immediately. But when Moses and Aaron went before Pharaoh and declared God's command to let His people go, the opposite happened at first. Instead of making their labor easier, Pharaoh made it even harder. He stopped providing the straw they needed to make bricks, yet he did not reduce the number of bricks required of them.

As a result, their suffering increased, and they eventually blamed Moses and Aaron for making their situation worse. Even Moses came before God and asked why His people seemed to suffer even more after he had obeyed what God had told him to do.

“For since I came to Pharaoh to speak in Your name, he has done evil to this people; neither have You delivered Your people at all.” — Exodus 5:23

At that moment, if they had judged only by what they could see, they might have concluded that obeying God had only made their lives worse. But they had not yet seen the fullness of what God was going to do. Their suffering did not mean that God had forgotten them or that His promise had failed. The deliverance He had declared would come in His time and in a way that would display His power.

And that is exactly what they witnessed. God acted against Pharaoh's power, displayed signs and wonders, and brought His people out of slavery. When they found themselves trapped before the Red Sea with the Egyptian army pursuing them, He made a way where, from a human perspective, there was no way.

“Do not be afraid. Stand still, and see the salvation of the Lord, which He will accomplish for you today.” — Exodus 14:13

The Israelites who once wondered why their suffering had become worse eventually witnessed the power of God setting them free from slavery. They could not see the whole plan when they first began to obey, but along the way they learned that the God who had promised to deliver them could be trusted.

Obedience to God can sometimes be like this. At first, all we may see is the difficulty, loss, or trial that comes with obeying Him. But we should not judge the goodness of God's guidance only by the difficult part of the road we can presently see. The Good Shepherd sees the whole path even when we see only a small part of it.

Sometimes we understand the wisdom of His guidance more clearly only after we have passed through the difficult part of obedience. Looking back, we may begin to see what He was keeping us from, what He was protecting us from, or how He was shaping us through the experience. And even when we still do not fully understand everything, we can continue to trust Him because our obedience does not depend on our ability to see the entire road, but on the goodness and wisdom of the God who leads us.

This does not mean that every difficult act of obedience will immediately lead to comfort or produce the exact result we expect. But Israel's experience reminds us that temporary hardship is not enough reason to conclude that obeying God is wrong. When His will is clear, we can continue to trust Him even when we do not yet understand what He is doing, because the God who calls us to obey sees what we cannot yet see.

👉 So when obedience is difficult, let us not use the difficulty as a reason to doubt God's guidance. As sheep following the Good Shepherd, we can obey even when we cannot yet see the whole road, because He knows the path and we can trust where He is leading us.

And above all, Jesus Himself is the greatest example of surrender to the Father's will. In Gethsemane, as He faced the suffering before Him, He prayed:

“Nevertheless not My will, but Yours, be done.” — Luke 22:42

This shows us that surrender does not mean the absence of difficulty, fear, or struggle. True obedience can take place in the very midst of these things. What matters is whose will we recognize as supreme. In the life of a believer, there will be moments when we must say to God, even when it is difficult and contrary to our own desires, that His will is the one we choose to follow.

Presenting ourselves to God, therefore, is not the loss of true life. It is the right response of someone who has already been given new life. We were not saved so that we could remain centered on ourselves, but so that we could live under the good and trustworthy rule of God. The Lord who asks for our obedience is also the Savior who rescued us and the Good Shepherd who laid down His life for us. As we recognize what He has done for us in Christ, it becomes clear why we can willingly entrust the life He has redeemed into His hands.

CONCLUSION:

The real question this verse leaves us with is not only, “What do I need to give up?” but, “To whom am I surrendering the direction of my life?”

As people who have been given new life in Christ, we are called to trust God and willingly place our lives under His rule. We may not always see where His guidance is leading, and there may be times when obedience is difficult. But we do not need to see the entire road in order to follow our Good Shepherd.

So today, let us examine ourselves:

Is there an area of my life that I once surrendered to sin but still refuse to surrender to God? My eyes, mouth, mind, hands, time, abilities, relationships, or desires—is there any part of my life that I still insist on controlling instead of placing it under His rule?

The parts of our lives that we once presented to sin as instruments of unrighteousness can now be presented to God. The eyes that once looked at what was wrong, the mouth once used for hurtful or sinful words, the mind once ruled by sinful desires, and our hands, time, strength, abilities, and blessings can now come under God's rule and be used according to His will.

We are not simply saying, “Lord, I no longer want to use these things for sin.” We are also saying, “Lord, I surrender every part of my life to You. Take control, and use the life You have redeemed according to Your will.”

May we learn to say, even when obedience is difficult and we cannot see the whole road:

“Lord, Your will still comes first. I surrender my life to You. I trust You because You are my Good Shepherd.”

In the end, the life that was once surrendered to sin has now been given new life in Christ. We no longer have to live under our former master. As those who have died and been raised with Christ, we can wholeheartedly present ourselves to God and trust His good and faithful rule.
